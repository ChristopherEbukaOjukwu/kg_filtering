# What the Table Shows

## H1 Effect-Size Results

| Filter | Δ Log-Units | Cliff’s δ | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| F1 | 1.720 | 0.636 | Survivors have ~5.6× more publications than dropped genes |
| F2 | 2.158 | 0.780 | Survivors have ~8.7× more publications than dropped genes |
| F4 | 2.465 | 0.835 | Survivors have ~11.7× more publications than dropped genes |

All three filters exceed the preregistered threshold:

```text
Δ ≥ 0.5 log-units
```

by approximately:

```text
3× to 5×
```

All three p-values are computationally indistinguishable from zero.

Cliff’s δ values between:

```text
0.6 and 0.8
```

represent very large effects by conventional standards.

A common guideline is:

```text
Cliff’s δ > 0.474 = large effect
```

Therefore:

```text
H1 is supported decisively for every tested filter.
```

---

# The F4 Result Is the Most Interesting Finding

The ordering of effects is important.

The largest attention-associated retention effect comes from:

```text
F4: protein-coding only
```

not from F1 or F2.

This matters because F4 is supposed to be a biotype restriction, not an evidence-quality filter.

In other words:

```text
F4 is categorically different from evidence-strength or multi-source filters,
but it carries a larger attention gap than either evidence-based filter.
```

---

## F4 in Plain Terms

| Group | Mean `log(pubs + 1)` | Approx. Publications per Gene |
|---|---:|---:|
| Protein-coding survivors | 3.80 | ~45 |
| Non-protein-coding dropped genes | 1.34 | ~3.8 |

This means protein-coding genes are much more heavily published than the non-protein-coding genes removed by F4.

The key interpretation is:

```text
A biotype filter can behave like an attention filter.
```

Even though F4 does not explicitly use publication count, it strongly separates high-attention genes from low-attention genes.

---

# Interpretation

This result supports the idea of attention-associated retention.

The filters retain mostly well-published genes even when publication count is not directly used as a filtering criterion.

The F4 result is especially important because it shows that a categorical structural restriction can still reproduce publication-attention patterns.

In simple terms:

```text
The graph is already organized around well-published, protein-coding genes.
```

So when we filter to protein-coding genes, we are not only applying a biological category restriction, but we are also selecting a part of the gene space that is much more studied, better curated, and more visible in biomedical databases.

---

# Methods Caveat

The F4 result should be interpreted with one important caveat.

Some of the F4 attention gap may reflect a measurement artifact.

Earlier mapping results showed that `gene2pubmed` coverage differs strongly by biotype:

| Biotype | Approximate `gene2pubmed` Coverage |
|---|---:|
| Protein-coding genes | ~97% |
| lncRNAs | ~20% |
| Pseudogenes | ~30% |

This means the publication-count measure itself is better captured for protein-coding genes than for many non-protein-coding genes.

Therefore, part of the F4 attention gap may reflect differential curation of publication counts, not only differences in underlying research interest.

A conservative interpretation is:

```text
F4 strongly separates genes with high measured publication attention from genes with low measured publication attention, but some of this gap may reflect differential identifier mapping and publication-count coverage across biotypes.
```
