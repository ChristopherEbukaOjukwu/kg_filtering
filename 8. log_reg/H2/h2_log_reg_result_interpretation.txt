This is the H2 result. Decisive in every direction.

## What the regression shows

**Three large 2-way interactions, all positive, all significant.**

| Interaction | β | exp(β) | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 × F2 | 3.30 | **27.1×** | Pairs passing F1 are 27× more likely to also pass F2 than independence predicts |
| F1 × F4 | 1.80 | **6.0×** | F1-passers are 6× more likely to be protein-coding than baseline |
| F2 × F4 | 1.55 | **4.7×** | F2-passers are 4.7× more likely to be protein-coding than baseline |

For your preregistered criterion (|β| ≥ 0.3 for at least 2 pairwise terms), you have **all three terms with β ≥ 1.5** — five times your threshold. H2 is supported with effect sizes that aren't even close to ambiguous.

**The likelihood-ratio test from main-effects to 2-way: LR = 83,609 on 3 degrees of freedom.** Under independence this statistic should be roughly 3 (its expected value if all interactions were zero). The observed value is ~30,000× larger than expected. Statistical significance is meaningless here — the question is effect magnitude, and the answer is "enormous."

**The 3-way interaction is small but real.** β = -0.73, exp(β) = 0.48, p = 0.001. This negative coefficient is interesting: it says the F1×F2 super-multiplicativity *attenuates* slightly when restricted to protein-coding genes. Mechanistically, this makes sense — among non-coding genes, F1 and F2 are even *more* correlated than among protein-coding (because the rare non-coding gene that passes F1 has typically done so via a strong single curated source rather than via cross-source aggregation). For the chapter, this is one sentence in the methods, not a main finding.

## How to interpret this for the chapter

**The F1 × F2 interaction is the headline.** Stacking F1 and F2 produces 27× more co-survivors than chance would predict. That isn't redundancy (which would give 100% overlap), it isn't independence (which would give 1× co-occurrence), it's strong positive non-linear compounding — exactly what H2 predicted.

In mechanism: F1 (strong overall score) and F2 (multi-source) both reward harmonic-sum-elevating evidence properties. They aren't measuring the same thing — F1 captures "has a strong anchor," F2 captures "has cross-source corroboration" — but they're partially driven by the same underlying property: well-studied gene-disease pairs accumulate both strong evidence and multiple sources, while weakly-attested pairs accumulate neither. The filters therefore concentrate together on a small population.

**The F1 × F4 interaction quantifies the protein-coding implicit selection.** β = 1.80 means F1-passers are over-represented among protein-coding genes by a factor of 6 vs. baseline. In your factorial: 99.7% of F1-survivors are protein-coding vs. 97.4% baseline. This is the "F4 is redundant after F1" effect in regression form.

## The right way to frame H2 in the chapter

You can now make a precise quantitative claim:

> *Under default Open Targets filter specifications (F1: overall score ≥ 0.5; F2: ≥ 2 data sources; F4: protein-coding only), pairwise filter interactions on the log-frequency scale exceed β = 1.5 in magnitude for all three filter pairs (F1×F2: β = 3.30; F1×F4: β = 1.80; F2×F4: β = 1.55; all p < 10⁻¹⁵). The fully-stacked filter cell contains 27× more pairs than independent filter action would predict, indicating substantial non-linear compounding rather than additive composition.*

That sentence is the H2 result. It's defensible, preregistered, quantitative, and effect-size-grounded rather than p-value-grounded.

## A note on F3

You'll notice F3 isn't in this regression. That's because — as the factorial showed — F3 alone removes 1 pair from the unfiltered graph. There's no variance in F3's per-pair indicator at this scale. To include it would add noise without information.

For the chapter, F3 gets its own short subsection: a methodological note that the unfiltered Open Targets graph is essentially a single connected component, so the "largest connected component" filter common in network analysis literature has effectively null effect on this dataset. That's a finding, not a failure. It tells you something about the structure of Open Targets — every gene-disease pair is reachable from every other through the aggregated evidence network. Worth a paragraph.

## What's next

H2 is done. The story is solid: filters compound super-multiplicatively, with F1×F2 the dominant interaction.

Run a quick Mann-Whitney test comparing survivor vs. dropped attention distributions for each filter individually. This gives you the per-filter H1 effect sizes to report alongside the H2 interactions. Takes ten minutes.
